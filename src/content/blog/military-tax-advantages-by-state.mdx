> **Important:** State tax law for military members is complex and changes frequently. The information in this guide is educational only — it is not tax advice. State domicile rules, exemption conditions, and SCRA protections interact in ways that depend on your specific facts. Before making any legal residence decision, consult a tax professional or the free military tax resources available through Military OneSource or your installation's legal assistance office.

Most service members know that BAH and BAS are excluded from federal taxable income. Fewer realize that the state they claim as their legal residence — not where their orders send them — determines whether their base pay itself gets taxed at the state level. That decision is one of the most controllable financial levers in military life.

## Why state taxes matter more for military than for civilians

Under the [Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (SCRA)](/blog/scra-mla-protections-new-service-members), your duty station state cannot tax your military pay if you're not a legal resident of that state. A service member stationed in California but with a legal residence in Texas pays no California state income tax on their military wages.

This means the relevant question isn't "where are you stationed?" — it's "where is your state of legal residence, and how does that state treat military pay?"

Different types of military income are controlled by different rules. Before looking at state-by-state exemptions, it helps to understand which rule applies to which income:

| Income type | What controls state taxation |
|---|---|
| Active-duty military wages (base pay, special pays) | Your state of legal residence (domicile) — SCRA prevents duty-station state from taxing |
| BAH and BAS | Generally excluded from both federal and state income tax under federal law |
| Spouse's civilian wages | Duty-station state — unless spouse makes MSRRA/VBTA election |
| Rental income from property in another state | The state where the property is located, regardless of your domicile |
| Investment income (dividends, capital gains) | Your state of legal residence — but some "no-income-tax" states tax investment income separately |
| Reserve/Guard drill pay | Varies by state — not always treated the same as active-duty pay |

For an E-6 with 10 years of service earning approximately $57,000 in base pay:

| State Income Tax Rate | Annual Tax on Military Pay | 20-Year Total |
|---|---|---|
| 0% (no income tax or full exemption) | $0 | $0 |
| 3% | ~$1,710 | ~$34,200 |
| 5% | ~$2,850 | ~$57,000 |
| 7% | ~$3,990 | ~$79,800 |

These figures are rough approximations for illustration — actual tax varies by filing status, deductions, and each state's specific rate structure. They don't account for base pay growth over a career, making the actual lifetime difference potentially larger. Your state of legal residence is worth real money.

## The nine states with no income tax

These states impose no income tax on earned income. Military pay — like all earned income — is completely untaxed by the state, regardless of military status.

1. **Alaska** — No state income tax
2. **Florida** — No state income tax
3. **Nevada** — No state income tax
4. **New Hampshire** — No tax on wages or earned income; however, other taxes (such as taxes on investment income or other non-wage income) may apply — verify current rules
5. **South Dakota** — No state income tax
6. **Tennessee** — No state income tax on wages (eliminated its investment income tax in 2022)
7. **Texas** — No state income tax
8. **Washington** — No state income tax on wages; however, a capital gains tax applies to certain investment income — verify current rules if you have investment income
9. **Wyoming** — No state income tax

If your legal residence is one of these states, your military base pay is entirely free from state income tax.

## States that tax military pay with no broad exemption

These states generally tax their residents' military pay without a broad active-duty exemption. Being a legal resident of one of these states while serving means you may owe state income tax on your base pay.

- **Alabama** — Military pay generally taxable; verify whether any exemptions apply for your situation
- **Arizona** — Verify current rules; some exemptions may apply
- **California** — California's treatment of military members is nuanced. If you are domiciled in California and stationed outside California, the Franchise Tax Board (FTB) guidance indicates you may qualify as a nonresident for that period, and your military pay may not be California-source income while you are stationed elsewhere on orders. However, California's domicile rules are strict — simply being stationed elsewhere does not automatically change your domicile, and California sets a high bar for establishing that you have genuinely abandoned California residency. This is one of the more complex state tax situations for military members. Verify your specific facts with a tax professional familiar with FTB guidance.

- **New York** — Some exemptions available; verify current rules

## How to change your state of legal residence

Military members can maintain their original home of record or change their state of legal residence. The key concept is **domicile** — the state you consider your permanent home and intend to return to.

**The process generally involves:**
1. Establishing genuine domicile in the new state — domicile requires both physical presence and intent to make that state your permanent home. Filing paperwork alone does not create domicile.
2. Filing DD Form 2058 (State of Legal Residence Certificate) with your finance office — this changes which state's withholding is applied to your pay, but it does not itself change your legal domicile
3. Updating your state of legal residence in DEERS
4. Updating your withholding elections in myPay to reflect the new state
5. Filing a final-year or part-year return with your previous state (if required)
6. Establishing meaningful ties to your new domicile state: voter registration, driver's license, vehicle registration, bank accounts, and — where possible — property or family connections

The SCRA protects your right to maintain your original domicile even when stationed far away. You don't have to change your legal residence just because you get PCS orders. Conversely, you can establish a new domicile if you genuinely intend to make a different state your permanent home.

**Domicile is not automatic:** Changing your DD Form 2058 and withholding elections doesn't change your legal domicile by itself. Domicile requires physical presence and intent to make the new state your permanent home. Some states apply strict scrutiny to domicile claims — consult a tax professional if your situation involves a state with complex domicile rules.

## Spouse considerations: MSRRA and VBTA

The Military Spouses Residency Relief Act (MSRRA), as amended by the Veterans Benefits and Transition Act (VBTA) of 2018, allows a military spouse to elect to use the service member's state of legal residence (domicile) for income tax and voting purposes — regardless of where the couple is actually stationed.

For a dual-income military family where one spouse earns civilian income, this election can eliminate or reduce state income tax on that civilian income at the duty-station state — but only if the spouse makes the election. Key conditions:

- The spouse must be present in the state solely to be with the service member on military orders
- The spouse must claim the same domicile as the service member (or, under the VBTA amendment, may choose the service member's domicile regardless of whether the spouse has ever lived there)
- The election applies to the duty-station state's tax — but the domicile state may still tax the spouse's income depending on that state's rules

MSRRA/VBTA rules are complex and state-specific in application. Consult a military tax resource or professional before applying these rules to a spouse's civilian income — the interaction with each state's withholding requirements varies.

## Common mistakes

**Assuming your legal residence is where you're stationed.** It's not, unless you've actively established it there. Where you receive mail and where your orders send you are not necessarily your legal residence.

**Not updating withholding after a legal residence change.** Changing your DD Form 2058 doesn't automatically update your withholding in myPay. You need to do both steps.

**Forgetting that you have a choice.** Most service members don't realize their legal residence is actually a decision they control. Many default to their home of record from recruitment and never reconsider it, even after moving multiple times.

**Changing too quickly.** Changing your legal residence has implications beyond taxes: voting, vehicle registration, hunting/fishing licenses, bar admissions, and professional licensing. The tax savings are real, but think about where you genuinely plan to live after service before establishing legal residence somewhere new.

**Not verifying what your state actually says.** This guide is a starting point. Tax laws change. An exemption that was partial in 2024 may be full in 2026, or vice versa. Before making decisions based on state tax rules, read the actual state guidance or ask a military tax resource like Military OneSource's free tax prep services.

## The bottom line

Your state of legal residence is one of the few financial levers you actually control in military life. Over a 20-year career, the difference between a state with a 5–7% income tax rate and a no-income-tax state can amount to tens of thousands of dollars in cumulative state income tax on base pay — depending on your pay grade, the specific state's rate structure, and how many years you're subject to that rate.

That doesn't mean everyone should rush to establish legal residence in a no-income-tax state. Where you plan to retire, where your family has ties, where you own property, where your professional licenses are — all of these matter beyond the tax question. But understanding the financial impact of your legal residence decision is the first step to making it intentionally rather than by default.
