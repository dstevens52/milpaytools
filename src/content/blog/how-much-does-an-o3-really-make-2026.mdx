O-3 is Captain in the Army, Air Force, Marine Corps, and Space Force, and Lieutenant in the Navy and Coast Guard. O-3 base pay for 6 years of service is $7,737.00/month in 2026, but that figure understates actual compensation significantly. Adding BAH (ranging from $2,175/month at Fort Bragg to $4,518/month in San Diego), officer BAS ($328.48/month), and the tax advantage of those allowances being excluded from federal taxable income, total compensation ranges from approximately $123,000 to $151,000 per year — equivalent to roughly $131,000–$167,000 in civilian pretax salary depending on duty station.

## Why is O-3 base pay only part of the real compensation picture?

If you're an O-3 with six years of service — a Captain in the Army, Air Force, Marine Corps, or Space Force, or a Lieutenant in the Navy or Coast Guard — your LES shows $7,737.00 in basic pay each month. That's $92,844 per year. It's a solid number on its own, but it's the figure most officers use when they compare their pay to a civilian job offer.

It's also incomplete.

Military compensation has three major components — base pay, housing allowance (BAH), and subsistence allowance (BAS) — plus tax advantages that most O-3s never sit down to calculate. When you add them all up, the number looks very different, and the gap between base pay and total value is exactly what makes a stay-or-go decision hard to read from the pay chart alone.

## How much BAH does an O-3 receive — and why does it vary so much by duty station?

Basic Allowance for Housing (BAH) is where compensation gets interesting — and where most comparisons break down. BAH varies by duty station, pay grade, and whether you have dependents. BAH is based on local rental housing and utility costs for housing profiles tied to pay grade and dependency status. Current rates are built around an expected member out-of-pocket contribution, so BAH may be higher or lower than what you personally spend on housing.

The examples below assume an active-duty O-3 with six years of service, receiving BAH at the with-dependent rate, not assigned government housing, and receiving no special or incentive pay. Depending on career field and assignment, special pays and bonuses may raise total compensation further, but they are not included in these examples.

For **O-3 with dependents in 2026**, here's what the actual DTMO data shows at three installations:

| Installation | ZIP Code | Monthly BAH |

*Source: 2026 DTMO BAH rates, with-dependent.*

That's not a typo. An O-3 stationed in San Diego receives $2,343 more per month in BAH than one stationed at Fort Bragg — just because of geography. ## What is the 2026 Basic Allowance for Subsistence rate for officers?

Basic Allowance for Subsistence (BAS) is a flat monthly payment to offset food costs. For 2026, officers receive **$328.48/month**. Unlike BAH, it doesn't vary by location or pay grade. BAS is intended to offset the service member's own food costs, not the cost of feeding their family.

Here's the part that surprises people: officer BAS is *lower* than enlisted BAS, not higher. Enlisted members receive **$476.95/month** — about $148 more. The two rates are set separately by statute and have been for decades, so the total-compensation math for an officer leans more heavily on base pay and BAH than an enlisted member's does.

## Why does the tax exclusion on BAH and BAS make military pay worth more than it looks?

Here's where most comparisons to civilian salaries completely fall apart.

BAH and BAS are **excluded from federal income tax** under 26 U.S.C. § 134. They're also excluded from FICA (Social Security and Medicare taxes) and exempt from most state income taxes. They don't appear as taxable wages on your W-2.

A civilian receiving the same amount entirely as taxable wages would generally owe federal income and payroll taxes on much more of the package. Your base pay is taxed; the allowances are not.

As a simplified illustration — a flat 22% federal rate applied only to the allowance portion — here is one common way to think about the gap:

**To match the economic value of an O-3 at Fort Bragg ($122,886/year total), a civilian would need to earn on the order of $131,000/year gross** — base pay plus the additional pre-tax income it would take to replace the allowances that aren't federally taxed.

**For San Diego ($151,002/year total), that illustrative equivalent is on the order of $167,000/year.**

These are rough illustrations, not a calculation of anyone's actual taxes. This simplified illustration considers only a 22% federal income-tax rate; payroll taxes, deductions, credits, filing status, and state taxes would move the figure in either direction. The applicable marginal rate varies by filing status, household income, deductions, and other compensation. A common way to weigh a civilian offer is to consider it against total compensation, not base pay alone — and to run the numbers for your own situation.

## What about prior-enlisted officers — the O-3E pay scale?

Officers who were commissioned after serving in the ranks — through programs like Green to Gold, MECEP, STA-21, or an OCS pipeline — may be paid on a separate, higher scale. Officers with more than four years of qualifying prior enlisted or warrant-officer service may be paid under the O-1E, O-2E, or O-3E tables.

At 6 years of service, the O-3E rate matches the standard O-3 rate. The prior-enlisted advantage shows up later in the grade: standard O-3 base pay caps at **$9,004.20/month** at over 14 years, but O-3E continues climbing to **$9,609.60/month** at over 18 years. For a prior-enlisted Captain or Lieutenant who spends several years at O-3, that difference compounds month over month. The [2026 pay charts](/calculators/pay-charts) show the full O-1E/O-2E/O-3E columns alongside the standard officer scale.

## How much government TSP matching are O-3s on BRS leaving uncollected?

If you're on the Blended Retirement System (which applies to anyone who entered service on or after January 1, 2018), the government contributes to your TSP on top of your pay — and those contributions come in two distinct parts.

For an O-3 earning $7,737.00/month who contributes at least 5% of basic pay:

- **Automatic 1% service contribution**: $77.37/month — added regardless of whether you contribute anything
- **Matching contributions (up to 4%)**: $309.48/month — dollar-for-dollar on the first 3% of basic pay you contribute, plus 50¢ per dollar on the next 2%

That is **$4,642.20 per year in total government TSP contributions: $928.44 in automatic contributions and up to $3,713.76 in matching.** An eligible officer who contributes less than 5% of basic pay leaves part of the available match uncollected.

Over a career, that uncollected match (invested at a 7% return) represents tens of thousands of dollars in lost retirement savings.

## Does an O-3 make more than an E-7?

In base pay, yes — and by a wide margin. At 6 years of service, an O-3 earns $7,737.00/month in base pay versus $4,843.80/month for an E-7 at the same point, a difference of $2,893.20/month. But the two grades sit at very different career stages: an E-7 at 6 years is unusually fast-tracked, while an O-3 at 6 years is on a typical timeline. Comparisons across the enlisted and officer scales are cleaner when made at similar career points and on total compensation, not base pay alone. For the enlisted side of this same breakdown, see [how much an E-5 really makes in 2026](/blog/how-much-does-an-e5-really-make-2026).

## What this means compared to civilian salaries

Most people think of "military pay" as whatever appears in the base pay table. Using that number alone, an O-3 looks like a solid mid-career professional — but the base-pay figure still understates the package.

Once you account for:
- **BAH excluded from federal tax** (worth $2,000–$4,500/month depending on location)
- **BAS excluded from federal tax** ($328.48/month)
- **TRICARE coverage** with little or no enrollment premium for active-duty families, though deductibles, copayments, and other costs depend on the plan and services used ([see what it's worth →](/calculators/healthcare-comparison))
- **Government TSP contributions** (roughly $4,600/year — the automatic 1% plus matching)
- Additional allowances and benefits that vary by assignment, career field, and family situation

...an O-3 with dependents is typically competitive with civilian positions paying $130,000–$170,000/year, depending on duty station. And that estimate doesn't include the military pension for those who reach 20 years — a decision many officers first weigh seriously while wearing O-3 bars.

## The bottom line

O-3 base pay is $7,737.00/month at six years of service. But nobody should stop there.

Your total compensation package — including allowances excluded from federal tax, healthcare, retirement contributions, and the value of job security — is substantially larger than what appears on your LES. The exact number depends heavily on where you're stationed. The calculator gives you a more complete comparison than base pay alone.
