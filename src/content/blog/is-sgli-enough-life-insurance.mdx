SGLI provides up to $500,000 of life insurance at $26/month in 2026 with guaranteed coverage regardless of health or military occupation — one of the most accessible policies available at any price. Whether $500,000 is enough depends on your specific situation: dependents, outstanding debts, mortgage balance, whether your spouse works, and how many years of income your family would need to replace. For a single service member with no dependents or major debts, $500,000 may be more than they need. For a family with a spouse, children, a mortgage, and one income, it may cover only a few years.

## How much coverage does your family actually need?

A common starting point is 10–12 times annual income. But the right number is personal: outstanding debts, whether your spouse would continue working, childcare costs, how long until children are financially independent, and your family's lifestyle all affect it.

SGLI allows you to elect any amount from $50,000 to $500,000 in $50,000 increments. The premium is $0.05 per $1,000 of coverage per month, plus a flat $1.00/month for TSGLI (Traumatic Injury Protection) — $26.00/month at the $500,000 maximum. TSGLI pays a lump sum for a qualifying traumatic injury, such as loss of a limb, vision, or hearing, separate from and in addition to the SGLI death benefit. FSGLI (Family SGLI) covers spouses up to $100,000.

## Should you get FSGLI coverage for your spouse?

The FSGLI premium is based on the spouse's age. The question to ask: does your spouse have independent life insurance, either through their own employer or privately? If your spouse is employed, their employer may provide group life coverage. If your spouse doesn't work outside the home, $100,000 may be inadequate for your family's actual needs — childcare costs, mortgage coverage, and income replacement while you grieve and adjust. Private coverage on your spouse's life can supplement FSGLI.

## What are the alternatives or supplements?

Private term life insurance can supplement or eventually replace SGLI. A healthy 30-year-old may be able to qualify for competitive private term rates, but quotes vary significantly by health, tobacco use, occupation, and underwriting class. Rates that don't increase for the life of the policy are a key advantage of buying term while young.

The timing matters: SGLI terminates 120 days after separation. If you wait until after you leave the military to buy private insurance, your health may have changed. Buying a private term policy while still on active duty — ideally 12–24 months before anticipated separation — locks in rates while you're likely in good health. You can hold it alongside SGLI without any conflict; once you separate, SGLI ends and the private policy continues.

Layering SGLI plus a private term policy during active duty is also an option for members who want coverage above $500,000 now.

## Keep SGLI in place during a deployment

Some private life insurance policies may include war exclusion clauses — check your specific policy terms. SGLI covers combat deaths with no exclusions. If you hold a private term policy alongside SGLI, keep SGLI active throughout a deployment regardless of what your private policy covers.

Before a deployment, also verify that your SGLI beneficiary designation is current. A designation set years ago and never updated can create complications for your family during an already difficult time.

## What happens to SGLI after separation?

SGLI ends 120 days after separation; it doesn't continue automatically. VGLI (Veterans Group Life Insurance) is the continuation option. If you apply within 240 days of separation, you don't need to answer health questions — regardless of your health status at the time. After that, you can still apply until 1 year and 120 days after separation, but you must provide evidence of good health and approval is not guaranteed.

VGLI is worth knowing about, but its premiums increase every 5 years and become significantly more expensive with age. VA publishes the current monthly premium for each age band and coverage amount on its VGLI premium rates page; compare those with private term quotes for your age and health. The longer-term math favors buying private term while young and insurable.

## When VGLI can make sense

VGLI isn't always the wrong answer:

**If you develop a health condition before separation:** If your medical profile makes you uninsurable at standard rates in the private market, VGLI's guaranteed-issue conversion may be the only way to keep substantial coverage. The higher premiums are a real cost, but having coverage at any price may be better than being declined entirely.

**As a bridge policy:** Some separating service members use VGLI to keep coverage during the transition while they sort out civilian life and finances, then replace it with a private policy once they've established civilian income and can go through underwriting.

**If you're within 10 years of expected need:** For a service member in their mid-50s with health issues who separates, VGLI's cost at that age may be comparable to private alternatives, and the guaranteed issue avoids the underwriting risk.
