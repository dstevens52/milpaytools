A VA loan assumption isn't new — VA loans have been assumable since the program began. But in a higher-rate environment, where buyers are looking for ways to access below-market financing, assumptions have become increasingly relevant. The mechanics are more complex than most real estate agents understand, and the entitlement implications matter significantly for sellers. Here's how they actually work.

*This is educational information about VA loan assumptions, not legal or financial advice. Assumption eligibility, timelines, and requirements depend on the loan servicer, VA policies, and individual circumstances. The assumption process can be complex — consult with your loan servicer, a real estate attorney, and/or a VA-experienced real estate agent before proceeding. MilPayTools is not a mortgage lender or real estate brokerage.*

## What is a VA loan assumption?

In a loan assumption, the buyer takes over the seller's existing VA loan — same principal balance, same interest rate, same remaining term. Rather than taking out a new mortgage at current market rates, the buyer steps into the seller's loan.

The key mechanics:

- The buyer pays the difference between the purchase price and the remaining loan balance (as a cash down payment, second financing, or seller financing)
- The original loan stays in place — it is not refinanced or replaced
- The buyer makes payments to the same servicer on the same terms
- The buyer pays a VA assumption funding fee of 0.50% of the remaining balance

**Why this matters now:** When a seller has a VA loan at 3.0–3.5% and current market purchase rates are at 6%+, the rate advantage can be substantial. A buyer assuming a $300,000 balance at 3.25% pays roughly $1,306/month in principal and interest. A buyer taking out a new $300,000 loan at 6.5% pays roughly $1,896/month. That's $590/month — $7,080/year — in payment difference. Over a 5-year hold, that's $35,400 in lower payments, which can easily justify the assumption process.

## Who can assume a VA loan?

**Both veterans and non-veterans can assume a VA loan.** VA eligibility is not required to assume. However, eligibility status significantly affects what happens to the seller's entitlement after the assumption.

To assume the loan, the buyer must qualify with the current loan servicer — credit, income, and debt-to-income evaluation. The servicer sets its own requirements, which may differ from what VA would require on a new loan. VA does not set a minimum credit score for assumptions, but most servicers do.

The buyer does not need to obtain a new Certificate of Eligibility unless they are substituting their VA entitlement for the seller's.

## What happens to the seller's entitlement?

This is the most important and most misunderstood aspect of VA loan assumptions. The answer depends entirely on whether the buyer is a VA-eligible veteran who formally substitutes their entitlement.

**Scenario A — VA-eligible buyer with entitlement substitution:**
The buyer uses their own VA entitlement to replace the seller's. The seller's entitlement is released upon assumption completion. The seller can use their VA loan benefit again with full entitlement — same as if they had sold the home and paid off the loan conventionally.

**Scenario B — Non-veteran buyer, or veteran without entitlement substitution:**
The seller's entitlement remains tied to the assumed loan until the loan is fully paid off. The seller cannot use that portion of entitlement for a new VA purchase until then. Depending on remaining entitlement, the seller may still qualify for a second VA loan — but may need a down payment to cover the entitlement gap.

> Sellers should strongly prefer assumption by a VA-eligible buyer who will formally substitute their entitlement. Without substitution, the seller's VA benefit is effectively locked to the assumed loan — potentially for decades — until it is paid off.

This is why it's critical for sellers to confirm the buyer's VA eligibility and entitlement substitution intent before proceeding. Accepting a non-veteran's assumption offer may preserve a higher rate advantage for the buyer while creating a long-term constraint for the seller.

For a detailed explanation of how entitlement works and how it is restored under different scenarios, see [Can I Use My VA Loan Benefit Again?](/blog/can-i-use-va-loan-again).

## What is the assumption funding fee?

The buyer pays a VA funding fee of **0.50% of the remaining loan balance** at assumption. This is the same rate as an IRRRL — much lower than a purchase loan funding fee.

**Example:** Remaining balance of $280,000 × 0.50% = **$1,400 assumption funding fee.**

Standard disability-related exemptions may apply to the buyer. Confirm exemption status with the loan servicer before closing. For a full list of exemptions, see [VA Loan Funding Fee Explained](/blog/va-loan-funding-fee-explained).

## How the assumption process works

1. **Buyer and seller agree on purchase price and terms.** The assumption must be disclosed in the purchase contract.
2. **Buyer contacts the loan servicer** to request the assumption package and begin the qualification review.
3. **Servicer evaluates the buyer** — credit, income, assets, and ability to repay. This is a full creditworthiness review, not a rubber stamp.
4. **Entitlement substitution paperwork** — if the buyer is VA-eligible and substituting entitlement, VA and both parties' Certificate of Eligibility documentation are involved.
5. **Servicer issues approval or denial.** The servicer — not VA directly — makes the assumption decision in most cases.
6. **Closing:** The buyer formally takes over the loan. The seller is released from liability (if the servicer grants a release of liability — this is not automatic and should be confirmed in writing).
7. **The seller's entitlement is restored** (if VA-eligible buyer substituted entitlement) or remains tied up (if not).

**Timeline:** Assumption processing typically takes 45–90+ days. This is meaningfully longer than a standard purchase closing, which typically runs 30–45 days. Plan for this timeline and factor it into any purchase contract.

## The gap between purchase price and loan balance

In most assumption transactions, the buyer must bridge the gap between the purchase price and the remaining loan balance. This is often the biggest practical barrier.

**Example:**
- Home value: $400,000
- Remaining VA loan balance: $270,000
- Gap requiring bridging: $130,000

This $130,000 must come from somewhere. Common options:

- **Cash down payment** — the buyer simply brings the gap to closing
- **Second mortgage** — a conventional second loan to cover the gap (lender approval required; not all servicers allow this)
- **Seller financing** — the seller carries a second note on the gap (complex, requires legal and tax consideration)

In markets with significant appreciation since the seller purchased, the gap can be very large. A $250,000 loan balance on a home now worth $500,000 requires $250,000 from the buyer — which effectively neutralizes the rate advantage for many buyers who don't have that cash.

The rate advantage of an assumption is most compelling when the gap is manageable relative to the buyer's resources.

## Why sellers and agents sometimes resist assumptions

- **Extended timeline.** 45–90+ days is a long closing for many sellers who need to move or close quickly.
- **Servicer complexity.** Some servicers are unfamiliar with or slow to process assumptions, creating administrative friction.
- **Agent unfamiliarity.** Many real estate agents have never processed an assumption and may discourage sellers from accepting assumption offers simply because the process is unfamiliar.
- **Entitlement risk.** If the buyer is not VA-eligible or declines entitlement substitution, the seller's VA benefit is constrained indefinitely.
- **Conventional buyer competition.** In a competitive market, a conventional buyer offering a faster close or higher price may be more attractive regardless of rate.

Despite these friction points, a below-market rate can be a genuine and significant selling advantage in a high-rate environment — particularly for homes priced in segments where monthly payment sensitivity is high.

## When assumptions make sense for buyers

Consider an assumption seriously when:

- The existing rate is materially below current market rates (typically more than 1.5–2.0% difference to justify the complexity)
- The gap between the purchase price and remaining balance is manageable with your available cash or financing
- You can accommodate a 45–90+ day closing timeline
- The monthly payment savings over your expected hold period exceed the cost and friction of the assumption process

**Payment comparison example:**

| | Assumption (existing rate) | New VA loan (market rate) |
|---|---|---|
| Loan balance | $300,000 | $300,000 |
| Rate | 3.25% | 6.50% |
| Monthly P&I | $1,306 | $1,896 |
| Monthly savings | — | -$590/month |
| Annual savings | — | -$7,080/year |
| 5-year savings | — | -$35,400 |

The $1,400 assumption funding fee (0.50% of $280,000 in this example) is recovered in less than 3 months of payment savings.

## When assumptions make sense for sellers

An assumption can be a genuine competitive advantage when:

- Your existing rate is materially below current market rates — it attracts buyers who might not otherwise afford your home at current rates
- You can find a VA-eligible buyer willing to substitute entitlement (releases your VA benefit for future use)
- The market is slow and a meaningful rate advantage helps your home stand out against competing listings
- You are comfortable with a longer closing timeline

And the entitlement issue: if you cannot find a VA-eligible buyer willing to substitute, you need to decide whether the sale is worth constraining your VA entitlement for the life of the assumed loan.

## When assumptions may not make sense

- **The gap is too large.** If the home has appreciated significantly, the gap between appraised value and remaining balance may price most buyers out of the assumption regardless of the rate advantage.
- **The seller cannot accept an extended timeline.** If you're purchasing a new home simultaneously and need proceeds quickly, a 45–90 day assumption may not work.
- **No VA-eligible buyer is available** and the seller needs entitlement released.
- **The rate differential is small.** If current market rates are only 0.5–1.0% above the existing loan rate, the payment savings may not justify the assumption complexity.
- **The remaining balance is small relative to the home value.** The rate advantage applies to the assumed balance, not the full home value. A $150,000 balance on a $500,000 home produces modest monthly savings relative to the gap the buyer must bridge.

## VA loan assumption vs IRRRL vs new VA loan

These are distinct transactions that apply to different situations:

**VA loan assumption:** A buyer takes over the seller's existing VA loan at the existing rate. The seller's loan is not paid off — it continues under the buyer's name. Seller's entitlement may or may not be released depending on buyer's VA status and entitlement substitution.

**IRRRL (VA Streamline Refinance):** The existing VA borrower refinances their own existing VA loan to a lower rate. Not applicable to a purchase transaction. Only available to the current borrower.

**New VA purchase loan:** The buyer obtains a new loan at current market rates. The seller's existing VA loan is paid off at closing. The seller's entitlement is fully released. The buyer uses their own entitlement (or available partial entitlement) on the new loan.

## The bottom line

VA loan assumptions are a legitimate tool — particularly in high-rate environments where the rate differential between an existing loan and current market rates is significant. The benefits are real: a below-market rate, a modest assumption funding fee, and potentially a major payment advantage for the buyer.

But the complexity is also real: extended timelines, entitlement implications for the seller, servicer qualification requirements, and the gap between purchase price and remaining balance that the buyer must bridge.

For sellers, the entitlement question is the most important: if you cannot find a VA-eligible buyer willing to substitute entitlement, accepting a non-veteran's assumption offer means your VA loan benefit is tied up until that loan is paid off — potentially for 20–30 years.

For buyers, an assumption is worth serious analysis when the rate differential is large and the gap is manageable. Run the numbers, confirm the servicer's process, and plan for the timeline.
