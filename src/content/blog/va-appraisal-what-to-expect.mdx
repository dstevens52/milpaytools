VA homebuyers hear a lot about the appraisal — usually from agents or sellers treating it as a complication. Most of the time, it isn't. A move-in-ready home in a normal market clears the VA appraisal without incident. Knowing what the appraiser actually evaluates, and what happens when something doesn't go smoothly, takes most of the mystery out of it.

*This is educational information about the VA appraisal process, not legal or real estate advice. Appraisal requirements, MPR standards, and timelines can vary. Specific property concerns should be discussed with your lender, real estate agent, and a qualified home inspector. MilPayTools is not a lender or real estate brokerage.*

## What is the VA appraisal and who orders it?

The lender orders the VA appraisal through VA's appraisal management system. A VA-assigned appraiser — not chosen by the lender or buyer — evaluates the property. The borrower pays the appraisal fee, typically $400–$800 depending on location and property type.

The appraisal serves two purposes:

1. **Property value** — The appraiser determines market value using comparable sales. This confirms the purchase price is supported by the local market.
2. **Minimum Property Requirements (MPRs)** — The appraiser checks that the home meets VA's baseline safety and livability standards.

Both conditions must be satisfied for the loan to proceed.

**The appraisal is not a home inspection.** A VA appraiser evaluates value and whether visible conditions trigger MPR concerns — they don't systematically test systems the way a home inspector does. A separate home inspection, arranged and paid for by the buyer, is the appropriate tool for evaluating a home's condition in detail. Consider ordering the inspection before or concurrent with the appraisal when possible — it surfaces issues while you still have the most leverage.

## What are VA Minimum Property Requirements (MPRs)?

MPRs are baseline standards the property must meet for the VA to guarantee the loan. They exist to protect the borrower — not to add unnecessary hurdles. The standards focus on safety, structural soundness, and basic livability, not cosmetic perfection.

Key areas covered by MPRs:

- **Roof** — must have at least 2 years of remaining useful life
- **HVAC, plumbing, and electrical** — must be functional
- **Water supply** — must be safe and adequate (well water may require testing)
- **Sewage disposal** — must be adequate and functioning
- **Lead paint** — on homes built before 1978, peeling or flaking paint requires remediation
- **Pest and wood-destroying insects** — some markets require a termite inspection; evidence of active damage is an MPR concern
- **Foundation** — must be structurally sound with no evidence of active failure
- **Access and egress** — safe access to and exit from the home and all rooms

MPRs are not a checklist of perfection. A dated kitchen, worn carpet, or cosmetic deferred maintenance does not trigger an MPR issue. The question is whether the home is safe and habitable — not whether it has been recently updated.

## What actually fails a VA appraisal?

**Issues that commonly trigger MPR concerns:**

- Peeling or chipping paint on exterior or interior of pre-1978 homes (lead paint hazard)
- Roof with less than 2 years of remaining useful life
- Missing or damaged handrails on stairs or elevated areas
- Non-functional HVAC, plumbing, or electrical systems
- Evidence of active termite or wood-destroying insect activity
- Standing water, drainage problems, or moisture near the foundation
- Exposed wiring or electrical panels in unsafe condition
- Failed well water test results
- Crawl space moisture or structural concerns

**What does not typically trigger a VA MPR issue:**

- Cosmetic defects: dated finishes, worn carpet, paint color choices
- Minor deferred maintenance that doesn't affect safety or habitability
- Older but functional mechanical systems
- Landscaping and curb appeal
- Kitchen or bathroom condition (unless systems are non-functional)

Move-in-ready homes in normal condition pass without significant issues the large majority of the time. Properties requiring significant repair before occupancy carry more MPR risk.

## The biggest myth: sellers won't accept VA offers

Some agents tell sellers that VA offers are more complicated or take longer to close — and occasionally sellers pass on VA offers because of this. In most cases, this reflects agent unfamiliarity with the process rather than an actual VA-specific problem.

What is accurate: the VA appraisal evaluates the same things any appraisal looks at — market value and property condition. VA loans close at rates comparable to conventional loans when both buyer and seller understand the process.

What is overstated: the idea that VA appraisals are harder or more likely to derail transactions. MPR standards exist and matter, but they aren't a bar that well-maintained homes routinely fail to clear.

Working with a lender and buyer's agent experienced in VA loans helps address seller hesitation before it becomes a problem. A strong pre-approval letter and a lender known for smooth VA closings can make a difference.

## What happens if the appraisal comes in low?

When the appraised value is less than the purchase price, you generally have four paths:

**Renegotiate the price.** The most common resolution. A low appraisal is a documented basis to ask the seller to reduce the price to appraised value. Many sellers will accept rather than lose the deal — especially if they need a fast close or the home has been on the market.

**Make up the difference in cash.** You can pay the gap between the appraised value and purchase price out of pocket. This is sometimes called an appraisal gap payment. The VA loan still funds the appraised value; the gap is paid separately.

**Request a Reconsideration of Value (ROV).** If you believe the appraiser missed relevant comparable sales, made a factual error, or underweighted market data, your lender can formally request a review with supporting documentation. The ROV must include specific evidence — comparable sales the appraiser didn't use, property facts that were incorrect, or clear market support for a higher value.

**Exit the contract.** Most purchase contracts include an appraisal contingency that allows the buyer to exit without penalty if the home doesn't appraise. Review your contract terms carefully before waiving this contingency.

A low appraisal is not the end of the deal — it's a negotiation opportunity backed by documented data.

## What is the Tidewater process?

Tidewater is VA's early-warning mechanism for potential low appraisals. When an appraiser believes the value may come in below the purchase price before finalizing the report, they trigger Tidewater — notifying the lender that additional data is needed.

The lender then has 2 business days to submit comparable sales, market data, or other information that might support the purchase price. The appraiser reviews this before finalizing the value.

Tidewater is borrower-friendly. It creates an opportunity to correct a potential undervaluation before the appraisal becomes official — not a signal the transaction is over. Not every low appraisal triggers Tidewater, and Tidewater doesn't guarantee the value will come in at purchase price. But it does give the lender and buyer a structured early opportunity to present supporting data.

If Tidewater is triggered, your lender should contact you immediately. Work with your real estate agent to pull the strongest available comparable sales quickly.

## VA appraisal vs conventional appraisal — what's actually different?

Both VA and conventional appraisals assess market value using comparable sales. The structural differences:

**The MPR layer.** VA appraisals include the Minimum Property Requirements check that conventional appraisals don't formally require. This is the most significant practical difference.

**Assignment method.** VA appraisers are assigned through VA's system — not selected by the lender or buyer. Conventional appraisals are typically ordered through appraisal management companies with less prescriptive assignment processes.

**The appraisal stays with the property.** A completed VA appraisal is valid for 6 months and is linked to the property — not the buyer. If a deal falls through after the appraisal, a subsequent VA buyer will see the same appraisal during that window. This can be an advantage (existing value documentation for a new buyer) or a constraint (if the appraisal came in low, a new VA buyer starts with that number in the record).

**Timeline.** VA appraisals typically take 1–3 weeks depending on appraiser availability in the market. This is generally comparable to conventional appraisals in most areas.

## How to prepare for a smooth VA appraisal

Most of the work happens before the appraiser arrives:

- **Get a home inspection early.** Order it before or concurrent with the appraisal when possible — it surfaces issues while you still have the most negotiating leverage.
- **Ask the seller to address obvious MPR items.** If peeling paint on a pre-1978 home, broken railings, or non-functional systems are visible during your showing, requesting remediation before the appraiser visits is reasonable and often worth doing.
- **Ensure utilities are on.** The appraiser needs to verify functional systems — heat, water, and electrical must be operational.
- **Ensure access.** All rooms, attic, and crawl space should be accessible. Locked or blocked areas can flag as concerns.
- **Have comparable sales ready.** If you're in an unusual market or property type, ask your agent to prepare recent comparable sales in case Tidewater is triggered.

## The bottom line

The VA appraisal serves a legitimate purpose: confirming you're not overpaying and that the property is safe and habitable. For most VA purchases on move-in-ready homes, it clears without incident.

When problems do arise — a low value or an MPR issue — they are almost always resolvable. Knowing the process in advance means you can respond quickly and strategically rather than react with confusion.
