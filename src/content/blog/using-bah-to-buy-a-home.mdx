Your Basic Allowance for Housing pays the same whether you rent or own. The question is whether the math works to put that allowance toward a mortgage instead of rent — building equity with money you're receiving regardless of your housing choice.

At many duty stations, the combination of a VA loan's $0 down and competitive rate brings the monthly payment close enough to BAH that the trade makes financial sense. At others, it doesn't. Here's how to work through it for your situation.

*This post is educational and presents data to help you think through the decision. MilPayTools is not a lender, real estate advisor, or financial planner. Mortgage qualification, BAH rates, and local market conditions vary significantly — consult a VA-approved lender and local real estate professionals for your specific situation.*

## BAH as qualifying income for a mortgage

Most VA-approved lenders count BAH as qualifying income at 100% of its value when calculating your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) and maximum loan amount. This includes:

- Regular BAH (with or without dependents)
- BAH differential (when receiving less than standard BAH)
- OHA (Overseas Housing Allowance) in international markets

The lender will typically want documentation showing the BAH rate — your LES (Leave and Earnings Statement) is the standard source. Because BAH is set by DTMO and tied to your rank, duty station, and dependency status rather than employer discretion, lenders treat it as stable qualifying income.

BAH continuity matters: if you're nearing a PCS or ETS date that would reduce or eliminate your BAH, some lenders will flag this in underwriting. A strong qualifying picture includes residual income beyond the mortgage payment — not just a BAH number that exactly covers it.

## The tax-equivalent math

BAH is excluded from federal taxable income. This means a dollar of BAH goes further than a dollar of gross wages when it comes to paying a mortgage.

**The comparison:**

A service member in the 22% federal tax bracket receiving $2,000/month in BAH keeps all $2,000 for housing. A civilian earning $2,000/month in gross wages keeps approximately $1,560 after federal income tax (22% bracket) — before state taxes. To generate $2,000 in after-tax housing money, that civilian needs to earn roughly $2,564/month.

This is one reason the VA loan + BAH combination is particularly strong: not only does BAH cover housing costs, it does so without a tax haircut. The mortgage gets paid with the full allowance, not a reduced-after-tax version.

*Note: BAH is excluded from federal taxable income and most state taxable income — but state tax treatment varies. The tax-equivalent calculation above applies to federal income tax only. See the [Military Tax Advantages by State](/blog/military-tax-advantages-by-state) post for state-specific details.*

## What BAH covers at different duty stations

BAH is set to cover the median rental cost for your grade and dependency status at your duty station. Buying with a VA loan changes the comparison — instead of matching BAH to a rental, you're matching it to a mortgage payment.

**A framework for thinking about coverage:**

| BAH Coverage of Monthly Payment | What It Means |
|---|---|
| BAH > PI + taxes + insurance | Positive cash flow — BAH exceeds all housing costs |
| BAH covers PI, covers most taxes/insurance | Strong case for buying — housing costs stay at or near zero net cost |
| BAH covers PI only | Thin margin — property taxes and insurance come from base pay |
| BAH covers 80% or less of PI | Likely renting is financially simpler at this station |

"PI" is principal and interest only. Total housing cost includes property taxes, homeowner's insurance, and potentially HOA dues or maintenance reserves. A mortgage payment that looks BAH-covered on PI alone may not be when full ownership costs are added.

**Example: E-5 with dependents at a moderate-cost duty station**

Assume BAH of $1,950/month (roughly the national median for E-5 with dependents in 2026).

A $240,000 VA loan at 6.5% (30-year): PI = $1,518/month
Property taxes at 1% annually: $200/month
Homeowner's insurance: $100/month
**Total housing cost: ~$1,818/month**

BAH of $1,950 covers all of it, leaving roughly $132/month. That margin is real equity building on the government's tab — but it's not a cushion for repairs, appliances, or unexpected costs.

## When buying tends to make financial sense

These conditions together improve the case for buying with BAH:

1. **You expect at least 3 years at the station** — transaction costs (closing costs, commissions, funding fee) typically require 2–4 years of equity building to break even
2. **BAH covers principal, interest, taxes, and insurance** — with a reasonable margin for ownership costs
3. **A disability rating exempts you from the funding fee** — eliminating $6,000–$11,000 in upfront cost significantly improves the math
4. **The local market has a reasonable rent-to-price ratio** — markets where rents are high relative to home prices are more favorable for buying
5. **You have stable family circumstances** — buying is harder to undo mid-PCS than renting
6. **You understand the PCS plan** — if you can convert the home to a rental when you leave, the long-term build can continue through multiple assignments

## When renting tends to make more sense

There's nothing wrong with renting on the military. These conditions favor it:

1. **Tour length is under 2 years** — transaction costs won't be recovered before you PCS
2. **BAH doesn't cover full ownership costs at this station** — high-cost markets often have large gaps between BAH and real mortgage payments
3. **Local market favors renters** — some stations are in areas where home prices are high relative to BAH and rents
4. **You're in the first 2–3 years of service** — less stable, more likely to change duty stations or separate
5. **The transition timeline is unclear** — approaching separation without a clear post-service location is a poor time to lock into a property
6. **Family or lifestyle factors** — frequent TDY, possible deployment, family situation mid-change — renting preserves flexibility

Renting at a high-cost station while banking the gap isn't a failure. It's the right call in a lot of situations.

## What happens at PCS

The mortgage doesn't move with you. When you PCS, you have three options:

**Sell:** If the market is favorable and you've held long enough, selling may generate equity you can deploy at the next station. Account for agent commission (typically 5–6%), closing costs, and any capital gains considerations if the home appreciated significantly.

**Rent it out:** If you can find a tenant at a rent that covers the mortgage (or close to it), renting allows the mortgage to continue paying down while you're collecting BAH at the new station. The practical challenges: property management (yourself or a property manager — typically 8–10% of rent), tenant quality, maintenance costs, vacancy risk, and whether your lender's terms allow rental use. Most VA loans don't restrict rental after you've occupied the property.

**Cover it from other income:** If neither selling nor renting works, you may be covering both a mortgage and a new rent/mortgage from base pay. This happens, but it's a financial strain that's worth stress-testing before buying.

See [How BAH Builds Wealth](/blog/how-bah-builds-wealth) for a deeper look at the rental property path — including the equity accumulation mechanics across multiple PCS cycles.

## What happens at separation

BAH stops at separation. The mortgage does not.

This is the most common way military homebuyers end up financially strained: they bought a home sized to their BAH, separated, and their housing budget dropped sharply while the mortgage stayed the same.

**Before buying in the final 2–3 years of service, build the budget around:**
- Base pay only (BAH is gone)
- Any VA disability compensation you expect to receive
- Realistic civilian income at the target location post-separation

If the mortgage payment is manageable on base pay + disability alone, buying before separation can make sense — especially if a disability rating eliminates the VA funding fee. If the math only works with BAH, it's worth thinking carefully about timing.

## Disability changes the math significantly

A service-connected disability rating has two effects on the VA homebuying decision:

1. **Funding fee exemption** — eliminates $6,000–$11,000 in upfront cost on most purchase loans
2. **Post-separation income floor** — disability compensation continues after BAH stops, making the mortgage more survivable at separation

A borrower with a 70% combined disability rating receiving approximately $1,716/month in VA compensation (2026 rates) has meaningfully more post-separation income than a borrower with no rating. That income continuity is a real part of the mortgage affordability calculation.

For the funding fee details and exemption rules, see [VA Loan Funding Fee Explained](/blog/va-loan-funding-fee-explained).

## The bottom line

BAH is a powerful resource for homebuying — especially paired with a VA loan's $0 down and no PMI. At the right duty station with the right timing, BAH can cover a mortgage payment that builds equity on the government's dime. But the math depends entirely on your grade, station, expected tour length, and post-service plan.
